Case Study · Work in Progress

Global Luggage Delivery Operations Transformation

Redesigning a multi-channel, multi-supplier luggage-delivery operation to reduce manual coordination, improve exception handling, and identify where deterministic automation and AI can improve speed, visibility, and service reliability.

Process Transformation Business Analysis AI / Automation Service Operations Case Study v0.1

Case objective

Start Customer places a booking through the company website or a supported distribution / OTA channel.
Current As-Is end Service is operationally completed and final delivery / collection evidence is recorded.

Success measures

Manual touchpoints per booking
Operations handling time per booking
Exception-resolution time
SLA adherence and customer-update latency

Baseline values are not yet established. No metrics will be invented; unknowns remain TBD until measured.

Current operating model

Dominant communication pattern
Customer↔ CS / Operations↔ Supplier / Driver

Drivers usually do not communicate directly with the end customer. Customer-service staff acts as the coordination layer and manually relays service-day status between both sides.

Typical examples

  • Customer reaches the airport meeting point → CS receives the update → CS forwards it to the supplier / driver.
  • Driver drops luggage at hotel concierge → driver reports status to CS → CS updates the customer.
  • Operational evidence and exception status are often relayed through the same CS layer.

Current-state process

StageAs-Is activity
1. Booking & confirmationWebsite bookings use an e-commerce platform with payment processing. Customers receive confirmation by email; a copy reaches the central operations mailbox. OTA/distributor bookings arrive through channel-specific email or backend flows.
2. Order normalizationDistributor / OTA details may need to be manually copied into the company’s own booking flow to create the internal booking record.
3. ValidationCS/Ops checks lead time, service date, address/product fit, flight/airport timing, and other service rules. Exceptions trigger customer follow-up, fee collection, changes, or cancellation.
4. Supplier dispatchOps checks the supplier list, forwards the booking, waits for acceptance, checks alternates if rejected, and may request/compare quotes for RFQ routes.
5. Pre-service QCOps checks order-entry accuracy, pending changes, supplier/driver confirmation, hotel details, and unresolved service risks. Missing customer details are collected manually.
6. Customer preparationConfirmation content provides service instructions and FAQ links. CS intervenes when customers need additional guidance, such as meeting-point or luggage-label instructions.
7. Pickup / handoverCustomer or hotel provides readiness information. CS relays necessary details to the supplier/driver. Pickup evidence is captured.
8. Transport & deliverySupplier/driver transports the luggage and records destination handover evidence.
9. Exception handlingPickup, waiting, delivery, hotel, address, flight, supplier and external disruption issues are manually coordinated through CS/Ops according to operational rules.
10. Service completionFinal handover evidence is recorded. Direct website customers do not currently receive a systematic owned review request; OTA reviews remain within the OTA platform.

Three primary bottlenecks

1 · CS as manual communication relay Customer and supplier/driver status is repeatedly passed through CS, creating manual touchpoints and potential update latency.
2 · Fragmented order context + duplicate entry Information is distributed across email, e-commerce, messaging, OTA and supplier systems. Distributor orders may require manual re-entry into the company booking flow.
3 · Manual supplier dispatch Supplier selection, forwarding, acceptance monitoring, alternates, RFQs, quote collection and some external bookings are handled manually.
Secondary control risk Existing handover evidence does not necessarily verify the correct recipient. A recent wrong-person handover demonstrates a chain-of-custody weakness.

Current-state evidence and controls

Hotel → Airport Driver confirms customer collection using a photo of the person collecting the luggage.
Airport → Hotel Driver submits a photo of the luggage tag issued by the destination hotel.

Bottleneck 1 diagnosis — CS as the communication relay

The current service-day communication model requires CS / Operations to bridge two different communication environments rather than allowing a shared operational status flow.

Customer → Email / Respond.io → CS / Operations → WhatsApp / LINE / WeChat group / Email → Supplier / Driver
Supplier / Driver → WhatsApp / LINE / WeChat group / Email → CS / Operations → Email / Respond.io → Customer

Representative updates

  • Customer requests a timing change.
  • Customer reports arrival at the airport meeting point.
  • Customer reports luggage has been deposited at hotel concierge.
  • Supplier / driver sends execution status back through supplier-side messaging or email.

Root cause

There is no single shared operational communication layer across customer, CS and supplier / driver.

Customer communication is concentrated in Email / Respond.io, while supplier communication is fragmented across WhatsApp, LINE, WeChat groups and Email. CS therefore becomes the mandatory translation and relay point between the two sides.

Operational implications

Repeated manual touchpointsRoutine status changes require CS to read, interpret and forward information.
Channel switchingStaff moves between customer-facing and supplier-facing systems during the same booking.
Update latencyStatus reaches the other party only after CS notices and relays it.
Context-loss riskInformation can be shortened, duplicated or missed as it is transferred between channels.

No quantitative impact is claimed yet. These implications will be validated later using actual message volume, handling-time or service data where available.

Bottleneck 2 diagnosis — fragmented order context + duplicate manual order entry

A normal KKday booking requires CS / Operations to reconstruct the order across multiple systems before it can enter the company’s normal execution flow.

KKday notification email → Gmail → KKday management system → Manual copy / paste → LuggAgent website / Ecwid order flow → Ecwid booking email → Supplier / driver

Representative KKday flow

StepCurrent As-Is activity
1. NotificationGmail receives a new-order notification containing the KKday order number.
2. Retrieve bookingCS logs into the KKday management system, copies the order number, searches for the booking, and opens the customer details.
3. Extract dataCS retrieves booking date, service time, city, service type, customer name, email, contact number, preferred chat app, hotel / Airbnb name and address, flight number, airport and terminal.
4. ValidateCS manually checks city, hotel address, service-area eligibility, meeting / service timing requirements, contact number, and whether any required information is missing.
5. Re-enter orderCS manually pastes / types the distributor booking details into LuggAgent’s main website using the Ecwid “place an order” flow, creating the internal booking record.
6. Generate internal booking emailEcwid sends the booking email to the LuggAgent mailbox and the customer.
7. Dispatch onwardThe booking email is then forwarded to the relevant driver / supplier for execution.

Root cause

Distributor order data is not natively normalized into the internal booking record.

CS must manually retrieve the source booking, interpret and validate it, then recreate the same order in the company’s own Ecwid-based workflow before the normal downstream process can continue.

Operational implications

Duplicate data entryThe same booking information is entered once by the distributor/customer and again manually by CS.
Transcription riskCopying names, dates, addresses, flight details and contact information creates opportunities for mismatch or omission.
Context switchingCS moves between Gmail, KKday and the LuggAgent / Ecwid order flow to complete one booking.
Manual validation dependencyService-area, timing and completeness checks rely on staff judgement before the booking can proceed.

This diagnosis is based on the current KKday workflow supplied by Zev. No time-saved or error-rate claim is made yet; those remain to be measured.

Bottleneck 3 diagnosis — manual supplier dispatch

For a normal fixed-supplier booking, supplier assignment depends on staff knowledge, a reference spreadsheet, manual email preparation, and later confirmation follow-up.

Internal booking ready → Recall supplier from memory or check supplier Excel sheet → Forward booking email → Manually remove pricing / branding / sensitive content → Send via Gmail / Google Workspace → Supplier replies “order confirmed”

Representative fixed-supplier flow

StepCurrent As-Is activity
1. Select supplierCS identifies the appropriate supplier from memory or checks the supplier Excel sheet.
2. Prepare dispatch emailCS forwards the booking email but manually removes information that should not be sent onward, including pricing, Ecwid branding and other redundant or sensitive content.
3. Send orderThe cleaned booking email is sent to the supplier through Gmail / Google Workspace.
4. Confirm assignmentThe supplier replies by email with a confirmation such as “order confirmed.”
5. Follow up if confirmation is missingOne day before the service date, CS sends a follow-up message in the supplier’s WhatsApp / LINE / WeChat group to check whether the order is confirmed.

Root cause

Supplier assignment and confirmation are not managed through a single structured dispatch workflow.

Supplier selection depends partly on staff memory or a spreadsheet, booking details are manually sanitized before forwarding, and confirmation status is monitored through email plus separate supplier messaging groups.

Operational implications

Knowledge dependencyCorrect supplier selection depends on staff memory or locating the right information in a spreadsheet.
Manual data sanitizationEvery forwarded order may require staff to remove pricing, branding or other information before dispatch.
Confirmation tracking across channelsInitial dispatch occurs in email, while missing confirmations may be chased through WhatsApp, LINE or WeChat groups.
Late visibility of non-confirmationA missing confirmation may not trigger manual follow-up until one day before service, increasing the importance of staff vigilance.

This representative fixed-supplier example confirms the T-1 non-response follow-up. The exact rejection-handling path is not specified here and should not be inferred from this example alone.

Task 4 — 5 Whys: Bottleneck 1

Problem: CS / Operations must manually relay service-day updates between customers and suppliers / drivers.

Why 1

Why does CS have to act as the relay? There is no shared system that lets customers and suppliers / drivers exchange operational status directly in a controlled way. A group-chat model, similar to some airport-transfer operations, would be too cumbersome at LuggAgent’s scale because it would create too many separate chats to manage.

Status: Why 1 confirmed by Zev.

Why 2

Why has a shared communication / status system not been implemented successfully? The constraint is not a single technical issue. Zev identified several connected barriers:
  • Cost and limited technical capacity: budget constraints reduce the ability to build and maintain a robust shared platform.
  • Fragmented supplier ecosystem: drivers work through multiple third-party last-mile and airport-transfer partners rather than one controlled workforce.
  • Lack of clear ownership: no single owner consistently carries the initiative from concept through implementation and adoption.
  • Incomplete third-party delivery: external development / implementation efforts have been started but have stalled partway, leaving partially completed solutions.
  • Customer adoption friction: requiring customers to learn another platform creates an additional education burden.
  • Low-frequency customer use: a dedicated app is difficult to justify for a service most customers use infrequently.
  • Driver adoption friction: drivers are often attached to external logistics / airport-transfer partners and may resist another tool or workflow.

Status: Why 2 confirmed by Zev. These are treated as contributing causes rather than forcing them into one artificial linear explanation.

Why 3

Why do ownership and implementation repeatedly stall before a solution reaches stable production use? The company does not have a persistent senior technology / product owner with end-to-end accountability for the problem. When internal hires or external software partners are brought in, they face a steep operational-learning curve because the workflow contains many service rules, exceptions, supplier differences and judgement calls that are difficult to understand without sustained exposure to day-to-day operations.
  • Ownership gap: there is no CTO, product manager or IT owner continuously responsible for discovery, design, delivery, adoption and maintenance.
  • Operational knowledge gap: new technical staff and external vendors must learn a complex, exception-heavy operation before they can design the right solution.
  • Resource constraint: limited investment in technical ownership and implementation reduces continuity and delivery quality.
  • Project continuity risk: technology initiatives can lose momentum when the people building them leave or external engagements end before the workflow is fully implemented.

Public-case-study wording intentionally focuses on governance, knowledge-transfer and resourcing rather than attributing motives or using internal personnel criticism.

Why 4

Why is operational knowledge difficult to transfer into a maintainable product or technical specification? The operating model is only partially codified. Formal rules do exist, but they are distributed across multiple spreadsheets and other tools, can change over time, and do not capture the full exception-handling knowledge used in live operations.
  • Distributed documentation: many service rules are documented, but across multiple Excel sheets and other locations rather than one authoritative source.
  • Changing rules: supplier requirements, service constraints and operational rules can be updated, creating version-control and knowledge-synchronization challenges.
  • Tacit exception knowledge: unusual cases and firefighting logic often remain in the heads of operations managers rather than in structured decision rules.
  • Limited frontline decision authority: CS staff can identify issues but may need escalation before making exceptions or policy decisions.
  • Part-time staffing model: many frontline CS staff work part-time and may hold other jobs, making deep operational knowledge harder to build and retain consistently.
  • Uneven destination familiarity: frontline staff have varying levels of familiarity with the cities, airports, hotels and travel contexts involved, so they may depend on managers for contextual judgement.
  • Supplier variation: service rules and requirements differ by country and vendor.
  • Fragmented tooling: operational information and actions are spread across multiple systems, channels and documents.

The public case study deliberately describes differences in experience and destination familiarity without tying capability to nationality or country of origin.

Why 5

Why does operational knowledge remain fragmented instead of becoming a maintained source of truth? There is no formal owner or operating mechanism for continuous knowledge management. Documentation was created by previous operations leadership and is updated intermittently, while the current operations manager is primarily occupied with live firefighting and retains much of the decision authority and exception-handling knowledge.
  • No documentation owner: maintaining operational rules, exceptions and decision logic is not an explicitly owned responsibility.
  • Firefighting dominates capacity: the operations manager spends most available time resolving live issues rather than codifying what was learned.
  • Key-person dependency: operational authority and contextual judgement are concentrated in one experienced manager.
  • No exception-capture process: unusual cases are solved case by case, but the resulting decision logic is not systematically converted into reusable rules or playbooks.
  • Tacit travel and destination knowledge: effective decisions often depend on accumulated travel experience and situational judgement that has not been translated into structured operational knowledge.

5 Whys conclusion

Root cause: the operation lacks a governed mechanism for turning live operational knowledge into maintained, shared and executable process knowledge.

The communication bottleneck is therefore not simply a messaging-tool problem. It is reinforced by fragmented systems, distributed supplier relationships, weak technical ownership, limited implementation capacity, and heavy dependence on tacit knowledge held by a single operations leader.

Status: Bottleneck 1 5 Whys complete. The wording focuses on structural process and governance issues rather than individual blame.

Task 4 — 5 Whys: Bottleneck 2

Problem: Distributor / OTA bookings have to be manually recreated inside LuggAgent’s internal booking flow.

Why 1

Why is distributor order data not flowing directly into the internal booking record? LuggAgent’s internal booking record is ultimately maintained in a Google Sheet rather than in a purpose-built order-management platform. Ecwid is used as the practical order-entry layer because it is affordable, already works for the operation, and staff are familiar with the workflow. Direct API integration from distributor platforms into the internal record is currently not realistic because LuggAgent does not have the technical capacity to build and maintain those integrations.
Budget-driven architectureExisting low-cost tools were retained because they solve the immediate operational need without requiring a custom platform.
Process familiarityStaff already understand the Ecwid → email → internal-record workflow, so replacing it carries retraining and implementation cost.
Technical-capacity constraintThere is insufficient internal engineering capability to design, deploy and maintain API integrations across distributor channels.
Spreadsheet-based system of recordThe downstream operational record is a Google Sheet, which limits native integration and structured workflow control compared with a dedicated OMS.

Status: Bottleneck 2, Why 1 confirmed by Zev. No assumption is made yet about whether each OTA exposes a usable API; the current limitation identified here is on LuggAgent’s implementation side.

Why 2

Why has the operation not moved from the Google Sheet + Ecwid workflow to a more integrated order-management system? Technology investment has historically received little or no dedicated budget. Available funds have been prioritized toward keeping day-to-day operations running, leaving insufficient capital for platform development, systems integration, technical ownership and ongoing maintenance.
Capital-allocation constraintOperational expenditure has taken priority over investment in technical infrastructure.
No protected technology budgetThere is no sustained funding pool for rebuilding the order-management layer or integrating external channels.
Short-term continuity over long-term leverageThe existing tools continue to work well enough to keep orders moving, so scarce resources are repeatedly directed to immediate operational needs rather than structural platform improvement.
Maintenance cost matters tooA new platform would require not only initial development funding but also ongoing technical ownership, support and change management.

Public-case-study wording frames this as a resource-allocation and investment-priority issue rather than assigning blame to individual management decisions.

Why 3

Why does technology investment repeatedly lose priority to immediate operational spending? The business operates under thin margins and recurring cash-flow pressure, which pushes management toward short-term operational continuity over longer-term systems investment. At the same time, there is no long-term technical leader with sufficient engineering depth to own architecture, integration strategy, rapid prototyping and ongoing maintenance.
Thin marginsLimited surplus makes it difficult to justify or sustain non-immediate technical investment.
Cash-flow pressureAvailable funds are directed toward keeping current operations functioning rather than building future technical leverage.
Short-term survival biasManagement attention is pulled toward urgent operational needs, so platform work is repeatedly deferred.
Technical leadership gapThe company lacks a persistent technical owner with enough engineering and applied-AI capability to design pragmatic low-cost solutions, connect existing tools, and carry them through to stable production use.

The public case study deliberately describes a technical-leadership capability gap rather than evaluating any individual executive’s engineering competence.

Why 4

Why has the company not established durable technical ownership? Beyond the general funding constraint, past investment priorities have leaned heavily toward customer acquisition and subsidized pricing rather than technical capability-building. When external technical expertise has been engaged, the company has also lacked a strong internal technical-buyer function to define requirements, evaluate vendors, challenge solution design and manage delivery quality.
Investment-priority imbalanceScarce capital has historically been directed more toward marketing, growth and subsidized pricing than toward durable technical infrastructure.
Weak technical procurement capabilityWithout a technically strong buyer, it is difficult to assess whether a vendor’s architecture, scope, estimates and implementation approach are appropriate.
Requirements-quality riskExternal technical teams may receive incomplete or weakly challenged specifications, increasing the likelihood of partial or unsuitable solutions.
Vendor-governance riskWhen nobody internally can independently evaluate technical progress, weak delivery can persist until the project stalls or fails to meet operational needs.

Public-case-study wording focuses on organizational capability: technical procurement, requirements ownership and vendor governance.

Why 5

Why does the business continue accepting a workable-but-fragmented tool stack? The company has historically been optimized for short-term survival and growth rather than for building scalable internal capability. Strategic attention and capital have therefore favored immediate revenue generation and operational continuity over long-horizon investments in systems, technical ownership and process infrastructure.

5 Whys conclusion

Root cause: the fragmented order stack is a consequence of a short-term operating model, not simply a software-choice problem.

The manual KKday → Ecwid → Google Sheet workflow persists because the business has repeatedly prioritized near-term growth and operational continuity over durable technical capability, while lacking the technical leadership and procurement discipline needed to modernize the stack effectively.

Status: Bottleneck 2 5 Whys complete. The public case study frames this as a strategic operating-model and capability issue rather than attributing the outcome to one individual.

Task 4 — 5 Whys: Bottleneck 3

Problem: Supplier assignment and confirmation still depend on staff memory, spreadsheets, manually cleaned emails and follow-up across messaging groups.

Why 1

Why does supplier assignment still depend on staff memory or a supplier spreadsheet? Much of the supplier-selection logic is not formally documented because the operation has never required every decision rule to be codified, and the underlying supplier conditions change frequently.
Incomplete codificationMany supplier-selection rules and practical preferences exist as operational knowledge rather than formal decision logic.
No documentation requirementThere is no enforced process requiring every supplier rule, routing preference or exception to be captured in a maintained source of truth.
Frequent changeSupplier availability, pricing, service areas, requirements and operating conditions can change often enough that static documentation becomes stale quickly.
Memory as working systemExperienced staff compensate by remembering current supplier behavior and using the spreadsheet only as a reference when needed.

Status: Bottleneck 3, Why 1 confirmed by Zev.

Why 2–5 — synthesized from the confirmed operating context

Why are supplier changes not systematically maintained? There is no dedicated owner or enforced knowledge-management process for supplier routing logic. The operations manager is primarily occupied with live firefighting, so changes are captured inconsistently and experienced staff compensate through memory and ad hoc reference sheets.
Why is knowledge ownership concentrated in Operations? Operational judgement and decision authority are heavily concentrated in one experienced operations manager. Frontline CS is largely staffed by long-tenured part-timers, but execution quality is variable and they have limited authority to resolve non-standard cases independently.
Why has the organization not built a stronger operating system around that knowledge? Cross-functional operational literacy is weak. Sales and Marketing are separated from day-to-day service execution, while there is no durable product / technology function translating operational knowledge into structured systems, rules and controls.
Why does that structure persist? The company is under sustained financial pressure and operates with little room for non-immediate investment. This drives low-cost staffing and short-term operational fixes while technical capability, documentation, process governance and system modernization remain underfunded.

5 Whys conclusion

Root cause: supplier dispatch is manual because the company has not built durable operational infrastructure around its supplier network.

The visible symptoms — memory-based supplier selection, spreadsheets, manually cleaned emails and cross-channel follow-up — are downstream of the same structural issues seen elsewhere: financial constraint, key-person dependency, inconsistent frontline execution, weak cross-functional operational ownership and near-zero sustained investment in technical capability.

Status: Bottleneck 3 5 Whys complete. Why 2–5 are analytical synthesis from Zev’s confirmed operating context rather than separately elicited verbatim answers.
